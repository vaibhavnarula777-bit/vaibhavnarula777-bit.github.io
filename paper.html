SNAPGraph NC

Modeling the Tradeoff Between Payment Accuracy and Access in North Carolina SNAP Recertification

Vaibhav Narula (Author)
Willow Spring High School, Raleigh, North Carolina
North Carolina Summer Ventures in Science and Mathematics, East Carolina University
Dr. Susan Mattingly (Mentor)
Abstract

SNAP recipients must recertify their eligibility every six months. Stronger verification catches payment errors, but it can also push eligible households out, and North Carolina's error rate is already above the 6% threshold tied to future federal penalties.

SNAPGraph NC models recertification as a directed graph: ten checkpoints, scored on complexity, documentation burden, and consequence, then tested against ten hypothetical household paths and four administrative strategies.

  • Income verification had the highest total risk and concentration score
  • The path combining variable income and work-rule screening carried the highest cumulative risk
  • Access-supportive targeted review, narrow review paired with household support, produced the lowest average modeled risk of the four strategies tested

Targeted review combined with household support, not broad verification, appears to offer the better balance between payment accuracy and access.

Introduction

Why a household can stay eligible and still lose its benefits

A household can be fully eligible for food assistance and still lose it, over a late form, a missing document, or a misunderstood rule. Every six months, SNAP recipients must recertify, and that process introduces paperwork, deadlines, and verification steps that can interrupt benefits before any fraud is ever found.

Table 1. How SNAP recertification features can affect payment accuracy and household access.

North Carolina's payment error rate remains above the federal threshold tied to future penalties. This project asks a narrower question: not whether recertification is burdensome in general — but which specific checkpoints in North Carolina's process carry that burden, and whether payment errors can be reduced without pushing eligible households out.

The 6% Line

North Carolina's error rate, the federal penalty, and what prior research says

SNAP exists to make sure eligible households can afford food. Over 1.4 million North Carolinians are assisted each month.

  • 7.36% — North Carolina's FY2025 SNAP payment error rate, versus a 10.62% national average
  • 6.0% — the federal error-rate threshold H.R. 1 ties to funding penalties
  • $420 million — what North Carolina risks losing in federal reimbursement if the rate isn't brought below 6%
  • Work-requirement screening now applies to most recipients aged 18–64 unless exempt

Reducing the error rate takes more verification, and that friction is not free:

  • Moynihan et al. (2015) describe administrative burden as learning costs, psychological costs, and compliance costs combined
  • Homonoff & Somerville (2021) found SNAP cases opened later in the month were 22% less likely to successfully recertify, even after controlling for eligibility
  • Potluri et al. (2025) found more support for recipients reduced food insecurity — the cost of staying enrolled matters as much as strict verification

Most prior research isolates one piece of this burden. SNAPGraph NC instead treats recertification as a connected chain of checkpoints, so risk can be located at the exact step where households are dropped.

The Model

How ten checkpoints, ten household paths, and four strategies were scored
  • Not empirical. SNAPGraph NC is a source-supported mathematical model, not an analysis of real case records — scores are modeled assumptions, not USDA measurements
  • Built in Excel, analyzed in Google Colab (pandas, NumPy, NetworkX, matplotlib)
  • Recertification is modeled as a directed graph: checkpoints are nodes, transitions are edges, and a household's experience is a path

Ten checkpoints, C1 (recertification notice) through C10 (outcome), were each scored 1–5 on three dimensions: complexity, documentation burden, and consequence.

Table 2. SNAP recertification checkpoints and modeled rubric scores (1 low – 5 high).
CodeCheckpointComplexityDoc. burdenConsequence Payment-error riskAccess-burden riskTotal risk

Payment-error risk = 0.5C + 0.2D + 0.3K
Access-burden risk = 0.2C + 0.5D + 0.3K
Total checkpoint risk = Payment-error risk + Access-burden risk

Complexity is weighted heaviest for payment-error risk, since complex rules create room for mistakes. Documentation burden is weighted heaviest for access-burden risk, since documentation gaps are what cause households to fall out of the process.

Ten hypothetical household paths were built, each scored 0–3 on six severity factors — income change, household composition, deductions, work-rule screening, document risk, and deadline/notice risk.

Concentration score = Total checkpoint risk × Path frequency

Four administrative strategies were compared by reweighting payment-error risk against access-burden risk:

  • Baseline recertification — no reweighting
  • Broad enhanced verification — heavier access-burden weight (+25%)
  • Targeted risk-based review — modest cuts to both risks
  • Access-supportive targeted review — targeted review paired with reminders, clearer notices, and help completing paperwork

Access-supportive targeted review had the lowest average risk (52.25) and outperformed every other strategy on every single path. Broad enhanced verification had the highest average (65.50).

What the Model Suggests

How the findings connect to prior research, and where the model falls short
  • Risk concentrates at checkpoints that are both difficult and nearly universal — income and document verification
  • Work-requirement screening has a high individual risk score but low overall concentration, since few paths reach it
  • The longer a household's path, the higher its total risk — which is why P7, the only path with all ten checkpoints, ranks highest
Broad enhanced verification produced the highest level of modeled risk because the increase in access difficulty was greater than the decrease in payment-error risk. Consistent with Homonoff & Somerville (2021), on how harder recertification reduces successful completion.

Access-supportive targeted review performed best in the model, echoing Potluri et al. (2025) on the importance of participation-friendly policy design.

Limitations

  • Coefficients are approximated, not measured
  • Path frequency reflects only the cases built into the model
  • Excludes human judgment, household-specific circumstances, technology, and delays
  • Sensitivity testing confirms stability within this model only, not real-world validity
  • Cannot establish causal relationships or predict future error rates

Future work

  • Validate modeled scores against caseworkers or SNAP participants
  • Apply the checkpoint structure to real administrative or case-level data
  • Compare North Carolina's process to other states
  • Test the model against a larger, more detailed set of household paths
